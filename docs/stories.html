From Python to industrial robots: a view from China's National Skills Competition

A student shares a glimpse of how programming, machine vision and automation meet in a national skills competition.

Shared by Zhaozhao Zhou · ARU–CJLU student

While participating in a National Skills Competition in Jingzhou, Hubei, Zhaozhao sent a field note from the event. He described contestants from universities across China and shared photographs of industrial equipment, robotic systems and practical workstations.

He highlighted three competition areas: Instrument and Meter Manufacturing, Industrial Vision System Operation and Maintenance (AI track), and Industrial Robot System Operation.

What stood out to him was a common thread connecting the different platforms: Python. The same language students use for exercises in machine learning can also help connect cameras, instruments, data and automated machines in practical settings.

The experience is a reminder that code is more than an exercise on a screen. It can become part of the machinery that measures, observes and acts in the world.

Research spotlight AI and Ethics · 29 October 2026

Beyond the AI slowdown: the Four As for governing frontier AI

Not all AI risks are the same. A new governance framework asks four different questions about what powerful AI should be permitted to do, who should use it, who decides and who benefits.

By Kirti Prakash and Ian van der Linde · Anglia Ruskin University

Read the paper in AI and Ethics ↗
Four ethical questions · Four governance responses

One AI risk framework isn't enough.

Rather than treating frontier AI risk as a single problem, the paper distinguishes four ethical questions that call for different forms of governance.

01
Action

Can humans constrain consequential AI behaviour?

Operational control
02
Access

Who can or should use powerful AI capabilities?

Capability access
03
Authority

Which decisions should AI make?

Delegated authority
04
Allocation

Who gains, who bears the costs and who holds power?

Systemic distribution

The central argument is straightforward: different risks require different forms of governance. Better technical control does not automatically settle who should have access to AI capabilities, whether important decisions can legitimately be delegated, or how benefits and costs are shared.

The framework also asks a second, easily overlooked question: can solving one AI risk create another elsewhere? Restricting access, for example, may reduce opportunities for misuse while also concentrating technological power. Good governance therefore needs to examine the consequences of its own safeguards.

With thanks to Larry Medsker for his help, and to Anglia Ruskin University for featuring the work.
